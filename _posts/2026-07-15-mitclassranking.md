Since I recently graduated, here's a tier list of all the classes I've taken at MIT! I'm not claiming this as an objective ranking in any sense, and factors in my ranking include how interesting the content was as well as class organization, logistics, and structure.

> Note: I'm trying to be harsher in these rankings, as I honestly enjoyed most classes I took. In reality, a few B's and A's would be upgraded to the A and S tiers, respectively.
> - S: Genuinely mind-blowing, transformative classes that changed my way of thinking.
> - A: Very interesting and well-taught classes that helped me really appreciate a subject.
> - B: Solid classes that were somewhat interesting or accomplished their purpose.
> - C: Mediocre classes that I didn't really enjoy but were not genuinely horrible.
> - D: Bad classes that were not enjoyable or valuable, but maybe had one saving grace.
> - F: Uninteresting and useless classes that did not provide me any positive value.
{:.prompt-tip}

At MIT, I completed a double major in 6-3 (Computer Science and Engineering) and 8 (Physics), with a minor in 18 (Mathematics). I also completed the MEng (Master of Engineering) in my last year, where I published two papers on efficient machine learning algorithms on custom hardware (FPGAs). Read more about my MEng thesis [here](https://aarushgupta.io/posts/kan-fpga)!

## ASE (Advanced Standing Examination)

I'm including tiers of all the classes I ASE'd out of.[^1] These are purely based on how interesting the content was, as I don't have much more to say beyond that.

[^1]: An ASE, or Advanced Standing Examination, allows students to test out of a specific class by taking an exam representative of the class's content (comparable to a final, for example).

**18.01 (Calculus I): A**

**18.02 (Calculus II): A**

**18.03 (Differential Equations): B**

**18.06 (Linear Algebra): B**

Classic introductory math courses, relatively interesting and foundational for future coursework.

**8.01 (Physics I, Mechanics): A**

**8.02 (Physics II, Electricity & Magnetism): B**

Classic introductory physics courses, relatively interesting and foundational for future coursework.

**7.012 (Intro to Biology): A**

I was pleasantly surprised by how interesting the course content was! I used to be a bit of a bio-hater, but learning about how we can manipulate the molecular structure of DNA through carefully designed proteins / compounds to perform cloning, sequencing, and other operations was genuinely mind-blowing.

**8.03 (Physics III, Waves and Vibrations): B**

While many of the ideas in 8.03 were extremely cool (e.g. diagonalizing linear vector differential equations to solve for normal modes), I really did not enjoy how much bash[^2] there was, and I found the applications somewhat unsatisfying.

[^2]: "Bash" refers to tedious mathematical computations or derivations, often consisting of lines or pages of long and nasty expressions.

**8.04 (Quantum Physics I): B+**

While the content of 8.04 is relatively interesting, I didn't really enjoy any of the scattering stuff. Thinking of states purely in terms of wavefunctions is also not a great way to do quantum mechanics (though maybe useful pedagogically for this course), so I'll have to reserve a better rating for Quantum Physics II :).

## Fall 2022

*Semester recap: one of my favorite semesters and definitely the most fun one I had at MIT! My workload felt quite manageable, especially since all classes were on P/NR our first semester.*

**8.033 (Special Relativity): A**

The first part of the course was very interesting, as it introduced a lot of the core special relativity concepts (time dilation, length contraction, various paradoxes, etc.) in a relatively principled manner, using tensor calculus to speak about invariants, vectors, etc. Later parts of the course went more into general relativity, including topics related to black holes; the lectures were really enjoyable but also a bit confusing. However, the goal here was likely to give us a "taste" of general relativity rather than a graduate-level treatment of the subject.

Aside from the interesting content, this class was definitely one of the best lecture experiences I've had at MIT: a few of us would go up to the professor (Netta Engelhardt) after lecture with a bunch of questions, which often led to interesting tangents and 10+ minute discussions. Psetting for this class was frustrating at times but also very enjoyable and rewarding, particularly because I psetted with a lot of friends.

**6.1200 (Math for Computer Science): B**

A standard introductory discrete math course: some of the number theory concepts felt fresh, but a lot of the material (combinatorics / probability, basic graph theory, basic proof techniques) was already familiar to me.

**6.1900 (Intro to C & Assembly): B**

I found this class difficult to tier-list, because learning and writing assembly is just fundamentally annoying. While it wasn't the most enjoyable, the class did its job in teaching me how to write assembly, introducing concepts like stack usage and calling conventions, and clarifying the primitives of C.

**3.091 (Intro to Solid State Chemistry): C**

Hard to comment on this class. I think the content was somewhat interesting, but I did not find it particularly engaging (which also led me to put limited effort into it given P/NR).

**21L.019 (Intro to European and Latin American Fiction): F**

I was actually excited for this class because I enjoy learning Spanish, but I found it to be extremely boring. A lot of the analysis felt forced, adhering to the English major trope of overanalyzing tiny passages.

## IAP 2023

*Semester (term?) recap: really enjoyed both courses I took, especially because I took them both with the same set of friends (which led us to hang out 24/7).*

**8.223 (Classical Mechanics II): A**

This class presenting some of the most foundational ideas in physics (principle of least action, writing down Lagrangians and Hamiltonians, symmetries, conservation laws). While some of the psets were filled with Lagrangian / Hamiltonian bash, that's a largely unavoidable part of learning classical mechanics and not something specific to this class.

It wouldn't be an overstatement to say that this was one of the first classes that taught me what it means to think like a physicist.

**Web Lab: B**

This class taught us how to build web apps in JavaScript / TypeScript. While it was quite well-run, it made me realize that I just did not enjoy web development.

## Spring 2023

*Semester recap: also a really enjoyable semester! It was still manageable, but I felt a significant ramp-up in my courseload, not only in the amount of work I had to do but also in the stress of actually caring about grades (no P/NR). In hindsight, this courseload doesn't even come close to some of my more brutal semesters, which is a nice marker of personal growth.*

**6.1010 (Fundamentals of Programming): A**

This was a really well-structured and interesting class! The labs included a lot of canonical programming problems covering various uses of recursion, tries, parsing, etc. that helped me become a better programmer. I came into this class thinking that I already knew how to program and that it would thus be trivial, but it ended up being an extremely fun and valuable learning experience.

**6.1210 (Introduction to Algorithms): A**

I really enjoyed the combination of proof-based discussion and creative problem solving in this class. Taking a seemingly intractable problem and designing an algorithm from core principles, or as a variant of an existing one, was satisfying in an almost Olympiad-style way. However, the problems were repetitive at times: for example, there were essentially just two or three tricks behind all of the graph search problems in recitation, problem sets, and exams.

**8.044 (Intro to Statistical Mechanics): A**

The content of this class was mind-blowing to say the least. Just by making assumptions about the density of states in phase space (based on quantum mechanics), we essentially derived thermodynamics from scratch using probability and statistics. The long and arduous pages of bash in the lectures and problem sets were well worth it for some extremely beautiful results (definition of temperature, ideal gas law, Bose-Einstein condensate derivation, etc.) and learning some cool mathematical techniques (counting states through estimating phase space volume).

My one complaint with the class is that probability and statistics should have been an assumed prereq, so we wouldn't have wasted so much time at the beginning going over basic probability distributions and theory.

**18.600 (Intro to Probability): B**

I don't have much to say about this class except that it taught introductory probability in a relatively standard way. The course structure, problem sets, and exams were solid and interesting, but the lectures felt unclear and messy.

**17.41 (Intro to International Relations): B**

While this class did give me an appreciation of the strategies and tactics countries use to interact with each other, a lot of lecture / recitation felt like formalizing relatively basic intuitions about politics and game theory. (This type of formalization can be useful, but my issue was more that it didn't lead to any *additional* insights.)

## Fall 2023

*Semester recap: this semester was defined by two S-tier classes (see below) but was otherwise okay — I felt somewhat acclimated to an MIT workload but still stressed and overworked.*

**8.05 (Quantum Physics II): S**

Since I've already used the word "mind-blowing" too many times: this class was truly paradigm-shifting, not only in my understanding of quantum mechanics but in my understanding of linear algebra itself. Matrices, functions, etc. are all just representations of abstract vectors, and this class emphasized that we can manipulate those representations and change basis however we want to make a specific problem easier to solve. I also really enjoyed the course's treatment of the Heisenberg picture, coherent states, and especially angular momentum and the hydrogen atom, which doubled as a gentle introduction to representation theory.

8.05 was what made linear algebra and group theory cool for me, and I'm actually learning about Lie groups / algebras right now in my attempt to self-study quantum field theory.

**6.1910 (Computation Structures): S**

I thought I did not like hardware before taking this class, but I was completely wrong. This class worked its way up to building an entire central processing unit (CPU), going from the digital abstraction for analog circuits to logic gates, then to sequential logic, then to pipelining, caches, and processors implementing an entire assembly instruction set! The pain of implementing and debugging RISC-V quicksort in 6.1900 (Intro to C & Assembly) was well worth it for appreciating the beauty of a circuit that can literally "execute" assembly instructions. The operating systems part of the class was also quite interesting and gave me an appreciation for virtualization, paging / TLB schemes, and other core OS paradigms.

Debugging the labs for this class was extremely painful (the hardest one took multiple hours in OH...), but they were very well designed. There was also an interesting "design project" at the end of the class, where we optimized a C program for neural network inference; it introduced some performance engineering techniques like loop unrolling, function inlining, inline assembly, etc.

I also TA'd this class and really enjoyed that experience!

**6.4212 (Robotic Manipulation): B**

This class was cool in that it introduced a variety of topics ranging from techniques for perception to inverse kinematics (calculating joint trajectories based on the trajectory of the robot hand, for example). However, the class largely felt like a collection of ad-hoc techniques rather than a motivated storyline, and the labs felt like more of a lesson in using and debugging the Drake framework than in conceptual thinking. The final project, however, was really cool: we built a robotic arm that could flip and land objects (inspired by bottle flipping)!

**6.3900 (Intro to Machine Learning): B**

I knew most of the content coming into this class, so I found it a bit boring, but the reinforcement learning topics at the end were interesting.

**21M.600 (Intro to Acting): A**

A very fun and experimental class that was a nice break from technical ones: it taught me how to be more expressive with my emotions, how to be mindful and aware of my surroundings, and other classic acting lessons. The only thing I didn't like was that a lot of other people treated the class as a joke, which unfortunately is maybe to be expected with MIT students.

## Spring 2024

*Semester recap: this ended up being a really fun semester for me both socially and academically! I really noticed my growth from freshman year here, as I handled a much harder courseload better than I had in Spring 2023.*

**6.1220 (Design and Analysis of Algorithms): A**

I really enjoyed this class, especially how interesting the exam problems were! Some of my favorite topics in the class included amortized analysis (aside from some cursed potential functions), online vs. offline algorithms, max flow, and FFT and polynomial multiplication. The only topic I disliked was linear programming, as the class focused very heavily on setting up linear programs / the dual formulation but not much on actually *efficiently solving* them, which felt incomplete. My main complaint is that I wish the psets had been harder: some of the pset problems felt uninteresting and uninspired, while the exams often contained very interesting and challenging problems that I would have loved more time to think about.

**6.1800 (Computer Systems Engineering): B**

The class covered interesting topics across computer systems, but I was not a fan of the class structure. The final project, which had us sketch out a high-level system similar to Bluebikes, didn't teach me much, and the exams were purely based on Ctrl-Fing notes. While I didn't gain a deep understanding of any of the covered topics, the class did provide a nice, high-level overview of networking, distributed systems, operating systems, and security.

**6.1020 (Software Construction): D**

This was by far the most annoying class I've ever taken at MIT. The assignments and exams were pedantic to a fault: the focus was on following arbitrary conventions subject to high-variance and often poor manual grading (e.g. points taken off for comments / specs not formatted exactly the intended way). This led me to spend excessive amounts of time obsessing over the labs without substantial benefit. I did actually enjoy learning about abstract data types, spec-driven software, and various design patterns, which is the only reason the class isn't an F.

**18.650 (Intro to Statistics): B**

A good introductory statistics course that presented its content in an interesting and principled way.

**21M.645 (Motion Theater): C**

This class felt somewhat boring and repetitive after I had already taken an introductory acting class. The three-hour sessions also made it particularly exhausting, especially because a lot of the activities and exercises weren't that fun.

## Fall 2024

*Semester recap: this was my HELL semester. I took 6 classes, several of which I found quite hard (e.g. 6.2050), and was also a lab assistant for 6.1910 and a grader for 6.1220. It was made worse by this being my first semester as president of my a cappella group, during which I got sick like 4 times. A heinous combination overall that I would not recommend. The silver lining was that I truly learned how to grind and also became significantly better at organizing and managing my time and work.*

**18.404 (Theory of Computation): A**

This class really reshaped how I thought about computation, covering topics such as automata, Turing machines, and deterministic and non-deterministic computation. A lot of the proof techniques in the class, especially the recursion theorem, were also very clever and somewhat mind-bending. Thinking about reductions as a way to define "hardness equivalence classes" across problems is a paradigm that has really stuck with me, even in a non-CS context.

**6.5940 (TinyML and Efficient Deep Learning): D**

This class was quite poorly structured in that the lab assignments were essentially "fill-in-the-blank" Python notebooks. The content actually spanned a lot of cool research at the frontier of ML systems, but the assignments and final project really did not help me engage with it in a meaningful way. It's also possible that I could've gotten more out of the class if I had made a more active effort, given how busy I was that semester.

**6.7960 (Deep Learning): B**

This class helped me formalize and structure a lot of my deep learning knowledge and also introduced me to unfamiliar concepts, including contrastive learning and generative modeling (variational autoencoders, diffusion, etc.). It is difficult to balance theoretical and empirical aspects of teaching in applied machine learning classes, particularly to design labs that guide students to cool results (e.g. powerful models and demonstrations) while also requiring them to think deeply rather than following instructions. Given my complaints with TinyML, I do think this class did a significantly better job of balancing theory and real-world empirics and implementation.

**12.410 (Observational Techniques of Optical Astronomy): A**

We got to conduct our own research projects through weekly sessions of driving out to Wallace Observatory and collecting data using some pretty cool telescopes! My project measured the colors of different galaxy types (spiral, elliptical, etc.) and even how color varies within a given galaxy. The class focused a lot on strategies for noise estimation and elimination, proper significance testing and statistical thinking, understanding telescope and equipment properties, and other skills essential for empirical astronomy. The communication instructor also gave me excellent feedback on presentations, which helped me improve my delivery.

This was a great class all around, both because of the late-night observation sessions with my friends and because my years-long interest in astrophysics was finally complemented by some observational astronomy.

**6.2050 (Digital Systems Laboratory): A**

This class primarily consisted of FPGA programming labs covering finite state machines, communication protocols (e.g. HDMI), and memory systems (e.g. external memory). While the labs were alright, the coolest part of the class was by far the open-ended final project, which we brainstormed and executed ourselves: my musically inclined group built an end-to-end, real-time autotune system. Working on this project was so cool because of how thoughtful we needed to be about defining our system requirements (e.g. sample rate, frequency range), which in turn affected our system design, especially how to parallelize I/O with various signal processing algorithms.

This class, building on 6.1910, made me fall in love with digital design and led me to do a UROP and eventual MEng on FPGA accelerators for neural networks.

**21M.361 (Electronic Music Composition I): C**

This class was alright but definitely had a bit of a "modern art" vibe, in that it wasn't focused on practical music production but rather on designing more experimental and creative pieces. This might have just been a case of misaligned expectations, but alas, I didn't end up loving the class.

## Spring 2025

*Semester recap: I took on 6 classes again this semester, with similar extracurricular commitments to the fall, but my class workload ended up heavily concentrated in 6.1100 and 6.5840, both of which I enjoyed. Overall, this semester was actually pretty fun, partially because of how bad the previous one was and the skills I gained from it.*

**6.1100 (Computer Language Engineering): S**

I cannot praise this class enough. Learning about how a compiler works and building one completely from scratch was a foundational experience in my computer science education. Along with 6.1910, this class helped me fully understand what actually makes code run.

The field of compilers also includes a lot of really cool theoretical frameworks and algorithms, ranging from clever graph coloring algorithms for register allocation to mathematical frameworks like lattice theory for analyzing dependency structures within a program. Through the class, I also gained a much stronger understanding of x86, effective x86 representations for things like arrays, and many performance engineering tricks.

Beyond being extremely cool from both a systems and theory perspective, writing the compiler (which was 10k+ lines of code) was a large software project that forced me to think deeply about design patterns, data structure choices, and code organization. I also used this project as a way to learn Rust, which I ended up really enjoying.

**6.5840 (Distributed Computer Systems Engineering): B**

Taking this class made me fall in love with distributed systems as a field; as a result, I worked on very closely related problems in my internship at Stripe. However, I wasn't a fan of the class structure: we spent 4 weeks building out Raft, which got boring and led to hours of braindead debugging. Additionally, we had to read a lot of papers for class but never actually engaged with that knowledge beyond cramming it for the test. I would have loved some more labs or challenging mini-assignments that led us to understand GFS, DynamoDB, Zookeeper, etc. in a more hands-on way.

**8.16 (Data Science for Physics): A**

This was a cool class that introduced me to a lot of data science techniques used in experimental physics, with a focus on particle physics (my Master's advisor actually taught it). It was great to see statistical principles I'd learned being applied in a very practical setting, which we did by performing multiple physics analyses from start to finish.

**21G.040 (A Passage to India): B**

A fun humanities class where we watched and analyzed movies (including some famous Bollywood ones like Lagaan) and other media to understand Indian society. The class was super interactive, with a different group of students presenting each week. The professor was great, and I ended up becoming friends with a lot of the other students!

**21T.102 (Voice and Speech for the Actor): A**

This was my favorite acting class at MIT, and it taught me so much about my voice: how to express emotion more effectively, the mechanics of projection and resonance, and how to tell a powerful story. The professor was extremely thorough and methodical, and there were a lot of complicated exercises that eventually came together in an almost profound way to improve our skills.

**9.00 (Intro to Psychology): B**

This was an interesting class that I could've put more effort into (I took it for the humanities requirement). I did really enjoy the neuroscience part of the course, where we learned about the different parts of the brain and their connections to the behaviors / processes relevant to psychology.

## Fall 2025

*Semester recap: I primarily worked on my Master's thesis this semester, which I really enjoyed; check out [my blog post about it](https://aarushgupta.io/posts/kan-fpga/) if you haven't already! Both my courseload and extracurricular commitments were substantially less demanding, making this a much more manageable semester than my crazy junior year.*

**6.S894 (Accelerated Computing): A**

I loved that this class focused not only on CUDA programming but on actually deeply understanding GPU architectures and their performance characteristics! The labs worked their way up from kernels for basic, element-wise computations to tiled matrix multiplications, design patterns like producer-consumer / double buffering, async programming, and communication primitives. While I realized that I find writing and reasoning about CUDA kernels somewhat painful, I really enjoyed and appreciated learning about the high-throughput SIMT programming model of GPUs.

## Spring 2026

*Semester recap: a chill semester to wrap up MIT, which also involved a lot of fun with friends! I finished working on the core content of my thesis relatively early, and I decided to take a non-technical leadership class just to try something new.*

**15.321 (Improvisational Leadership): A**

Since this was my first and only Sloan (business) class at MIT, I wasn't sure what to expect, but I ended up enjoying it! The professor presented a lot of different frameworks for topics like mutually beneficial negotiation, understanding and responding to what others need, navigating complex dynamics in professional settings, and more. We would have to write long (often 10+ page) reflections about our experiences in the class, which heightened my self-awareness of the aspects of leadership and communication I need to improve on. My main hope is that I'll actually be able to apply these lessons in real-world settings years after taking the course.
