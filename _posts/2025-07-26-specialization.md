Welcome to my first blog post! I plan to use this blog to share my thoughts about whatever topics come to mind, technical or non-technical. I hope you enjoy!

For almost my entire life, the idea of "specialization" has been instilled in me: I was frequently told to find something I really enjoy and invest every single fiber of my being into becoming the best at it. As time progresses, this philosophy only continues to become more pervasive: more and more children and young adults are honing in on highly specific niches, ranging from fields of research to specific career paths. While I did pursue my passions in STEM fields like physics, computer science, and mathematics from an early age, I did not specialize nearly to this extent while growing up. As such, I've recently been thinking a lot about _what level of specialization_ is "optimal". (I put optimal in quotes because the objective which "optimal" is being measured with respect to is unclear.)

There are a few points which this blog post _will not discuss_. Firstly, the case for hyperspecialization impacting socioemotional development, burnout, etc. has already been laid out many times, and I thus will not focus on this in this post. Secondly, I will not address short-term development in a specific area or field, as it is relatively clear that specialization would improve this.

Instead, I'll ask what I think is a much harder to answer question: **does early or life-long specialization in a field provide substantial benefit in pursuing that field long term?** I'll focus specifically on technical fields involving high-level thinking, as these are the areas in which I have the most insight. At first, the answer to this question might seem like an obvious yes, but here are some points which may make you reconsider. Note that this post is anecdotal and based off of my observations and opinions.

### The growth plateau and its connection to baseline thinking systems

The idea of a "growth plateau" refers to the fact that as you gain more experience in a field, there's eventually a point where you understand the core essence of that field; further breakthroughs or transformations in your understanding will require significantly more effort. Hitting this plateau is an indicator of substantial skill in a field, something definitely worth pursuing. However, there is a certain sweet spot after which the marginal level of skills and expertise acquired in an already-strong area is not as valuable as taking on challenges in other areas.

_Note_: While this sweet spot might take many years to reach over an entire career, a lack of real-world experience often creates an earlier, more pronounced plateau for college students and younger. The existence and strength of a growth plateau also depends on how broad a field is: for instance, if computer science is considered a "field", there is almost no growth plateau due to the presence of countless interconnected ideas.

I think about "intelligence" in two components: a baseline system for information processing combined with added skills and knowledge within specific fields. The baseline system primarily consists of a set of paradigms — whenever one receives new information or learns something new, it can be processed through one or more paradigms. One simple example of this comes from physics: one can ingest a particular theorem through visual representations (e.g. imagining the corresponding physical process) or through a more mathematical and rigorous framework (e.g. intuiting the logical, abstract steps that imply that result). These paradigms are both very useful, with either one working more effectively for solving certain kinds of problems. A similar idea applies when discussing paradigms across fields, with the key difference that paradigms are much less clearly defined.

While delving deeper into a field beyond this "growth plateau" can still lead to development, it may yield marginal improvements to the core paradigms developed and thus the deeper, baseline thinking system. It is clearly important to balance these two systems comprising "intelligence", which hyperspecialization fails to do.

### The practical utility of different paradigms

I'm currently reading [_The Dream Machine_](https://www.amazon.com/Dream-Machine-M-Mitchell-Waldrop/dp/1732265119), which covers the history of computing and the central figures behind its revolutionary story, with a particular focus on J. C. R. Licklider. Licklider, or "Lick", was passionate about and excellent in fields ranging from psychology to physics and mathematics and electrical engineering. That diverse expertise allowed Lick to define his lasting legacy as not only someone who made computing possible, but defined what the word "computing" even meant.

Lick's ideas were revolutionary through their focus on human-computer interaction. Specifically, his deep background in psychology enabled him to move beyond the typical understanding of computers as mere computation machines. Early computers were designed as single-purpose machines to solve tasks: for instance, the differential analyzer was used to solve specific differential equations. Lick was a key figure in making computing "interactive": he drew connections between how humans and machines operate, such as in his work "Man-Computer Symbiosis". His key revelation was that the _interface_ through which humans interact with machines is essential, one he only came across due to his pyschology-oriented thought process.

While working on projects like [SAGE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-Automatic_Ground_Environment) and [Project MAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_Computer_Science_and_Artificial_Intelligence_Laboratory#Project_MAC), Lick had a particular focus on enabling effective human interaction with computers; many "pure" computer scientists did not feel so passionately about this aspect of computing. For example, Lick was a key figure in ensuring radar operators, who needed to make fast, accurate decisions, could easily understand information displayed on computers in the SAGE Project. Lick's prior work in psychoacoustics likely gave him the tools to even understand information processing on a human level, while others in the group were primarily focused on the purely technological components of the system.

Reading about Lick's story is actually what inspired me to write this blog post: almost all of the most influential and revolutionary thinkers of any time period deeply engaged with many different fields. This is not a coincidence: they were _genuinely curious_ about the world around them, so it would only be natural that their interests were varied. Subsequently, their revolutionary work often consisted of connecting distant ideas together. This applies from old times to new times alike: Newton was the first person to fully explore a rigorous and mathematical description of the physical world, which was previously thought to be governed by abstract ideas and rules. Today, many cutting edge research fields bring together ideas from different disciplines (e.g. [state-space models](https://huggingface.co/blog/lbourdois/get-on-the-ssm-train) were inspired by control theory).

Formulating that observation in terms of paradigms, what has allowed for breakthroughs in many fields is examining a problem through a paradigm from a completely different field, one that was not previously sufficiently explored or thought about. Thus, extreme specialization in a field can sometimes suffer from disadvantages, even for work within that field, as compared a broader breadth of experiences; the advantage of building up one's paradigms can often outmatch that of building up skills while at the growth plateau.

### Improving at your weaknesses

Whether you should focus on making your strengths stronger or improving on your weaknesses is a controversial subject: some people argue that real growth comes from the latter, while some argue that you should find a path which hones your strengths and doesn't at all touch your weaknesses.

The actual utility of either approach is subjective, so one thing to focus on is the effects of strengthening weaknesses on one's personality and attitude. While it takes more raw effort to improve one's strengths, the amount of willpower and determination needed to improve a weakness is much higher: the process often involves putting oneself in uncomfortable situations while practicing unfamiliar and untrained skills, which can be demoralizing. Developing a mentality which emphasizes the necessity of these processes builds determination, character, and openness in a way that only focusing on one's strengths doesn't.

This relates to a generally more humanistic perspective on self-development: rather than solely optimizing for career goals, well-roundedness is crucial for a healthy sense of self which carries over into all areas of life. The skills and mindset acquired from this approach can be more beneficial than following a fully linear path of hyper-specialization.

### Why is specialization so touted?

A lot of the criticism for focusing on well-roundedness rather than extremely specialization comes from a career perspective. Specifically, it's important to find a niche one is passionate about and simultaneously talented at, and then spend significant time honing skills in that niche — the phrase "jack of all trades, master of none" is a classic example of this idea. However, this philosophy becomes problematic when applied during one's childhood for the reasons already discussed in this post, especially in fields like the sciences and humanities which require high-level and abstract thinking.

One large part of why specialization is ever-increasingly pushed onto young children is the college application process. As an increasing number of accolades and impressive feats are necessary for admission to top colleges, students are pigeonholing themselves into specific niches to stand out. For instance, high schoolers taking on software engineering internships, which is now increasingly common, was a rare occurrence when I was in high school. Students are not at fault: college admissions are an obvious goal to optimize for, and one would be at a severe disadvantage ignoring that reality. While real change needs to come at a systemic level, it is the responsibility of both children and parents to create an environment that fosters curiosity and diverse exploration — this is what children naturally want to do.

Anecdotally, some of my fondest childhood memories are of the projects I took on, inspired by going down rabbit holes of curiosity. These projects weren't built with a specific goal in mind or even to develop a certain skill. Instead, they were inspired by whatever I imagined at the moment, which included everything from basic, arcade-style video games to Arduino systems (a WiFi-powered lamp was my "capstone" project) to various electromechanical contraptions. These seemingly random endeavors didn't perfectly align with my current path, but they were invaluable in shaping my thought processes; more importantly, they sparked the curiosity that made learning so fun and exciting. While it is hard not to be extremely impressed by seemingly younger and younger children who excel in fields as specific as neuroscience research and machine learning subdomains, a key question arises: are these children missing out on the fundamental, play-oriented nature of growing up and learning? Does their extreme specialization come at the cost of well-rounded, organic skills and more general thinking systems?

For those in college, taking initiative to develop a well-rounded set of interests and knowledge is more feasible. As a computer science student, I'm glad I took courses and worked on projects in a wide variety of areas ranging from machine learning to systems engineering to algorithms and theoretical computer science — there are many more interconnections between these fields than one would initially realize. Even studying physics has augmented the way I process ideas and information, allowing for distinct paradigms that have helped me digest CS topics in different ways. These insights are applicable to almost all majors, and even across majors. While extreme specialization may produce short-term career advantages, a broader skillset is crucial in the long run.

To summarize, college admissions and early career systems strongly incentivize specialization. This is somewhat unavoidable, as benchmarks of success within these systems often prioritize aggressive, short-term development that is typically sustained only through high levels of specialization.

### A defense of specialization

To clarify, specialization is extremely important: exploring the basics of one field for a few days or weeks and then just moving on does not allow for any actual in-depth understanding or intellectual stimulation. These processes are essential for learning, and it is thus important to commit time to deeply learn and explore the nuances of a field. However, on the scale of years or one's entire youth, specializing in a broader set of fields is extremely beneficial.

The key issue is rather that society, especially in technical fields, is progressing too far towards one extreme on a spectrum of specialization versus well-roundedness. While specializing in a field teaches deep knowledge required to work on the frontier of that field, having broader insights allows one to draw connections, break out of "standard" ways of thinking about a problem, and generally be more creative. Working in different roles and types of projects, even if within the same general field, can also cut through the monotony of standard patterns of thinking and approaching problems.

### Conclusion

While there's no definitive answer to whether specialization in a certain field is "optimal", it's worth reconsidering the view that specialization is always the way to go. Hyperspecialization, beyond not necessarily being beneficial for long-term development in a field, can erode the curiosity, passion, and deep thinking that are central to meaningful learning and exploring, arguably the most important traits of all. These qualities are essential for intellectual growth, and it is important to not sacrifice them in the pursuit of excellence.
