Over the past few years, everything in my life has felt fast. As a (self-classified) ambitious person, I’ve always strived to take on more and more. This mentality peaked last year: I took six classes per semester, served as president of my a cappella group, and worked on course staff for two classes. As a result, I hand-built an optimized system for efficient multitasking and getting things done.

The past few months have been different. I’m taking fewer classes and focusing on my research. A lack of constant deadlines has made me more curious, able to focus for longer, and more thoughtful and intentional. This has prompted me to reflect on “slowing down.”

### A mental model

Human brains are not machines: we can’t instantly process information or mechanistically execute tasks. Yet it is our creativity, imagination, and craft that enable unparalleled abilities in learning and innovation.

These qualities take years to develop across childhood and adulthood. We don’t learn through programmable instructions, but through the slow accumulation of experiences that reshape our messy brains.

Yet there has never been a time in history when we’ve been expected to learn or do things as fast as we are today. While the pursuit of speed is rooted in ambition, it degrades the core human advantage in an attempt to turn us into productivity machines.

### Tasks vs. processes

Human progress is made up of *processes*, or the intricate, messy happenings that occur when we learn and do things.  *Tasks* show up on a to-do list. They're either done, or not done, like ticking a checkbox. 

**The fundamental issue with "productivity-maxxing" is treating *processes* as *tasks*.**

This approach works well for filling out a form, but horribly for learning, writing or painting. Unfortunately, the task-oriented mentality is everywhere. *Multitasking* is a prime example: you juggle multiple processes at once, just to finish them faster while sacrificing quality. 

During particularly stressful weeks of college, I would cycle between assignments, immediately switching problems when the current one required deep thought. I completed my assignments, but often only partially understood the higher-level ideas those very assignments intended to teach me.

Being stuck in this mentality leads you to undervalue crucial intangible processes. It’s easy to solve a problem without understanding why a different approach wouldn’t work. In contrast to the previous example, my currently slower pace of learning has allowed me to focus on the principles and intuition behind concepts, rather than just their mechanical workings.

Slowing down intentionally lets you invest in strong processes without feeling guilty about efficiency.

### Information overload

*Learning* is one type of crucial process that requires care and attention. Thoughtful learning involves:
- asking deeper questions,
- building first-principles understanding,
- marinating on complex concepts for hours, and
- drawing connections across domains.

**Crucially, consuming information isn't learning.** Rapid information consumption is doomscrolling disguised as productivity.

I realized this in trying to keep up with machine learning in 2025, skimming countless blogs and research papers and learning almost nothing in the process. In contrast, I've now shifted my focus to fundamental understanding: I'm currently travelling 5+ years into the past and implementing GPT-2 from scratch. While less flashy, this project has dramatically improved my understanding of concepts I thought I already understood.

Information consumption is tempting because it’s easy: you don’t have to wrestle with new ideas while skimming an article, watching a video, or scrolling through educational social media posts. Learning quickly offers the illusion of understanding while bypassing all of the messy, slow and complicated processes that build strong foundations.

### *Slow and intentional* practice makes perfect

These same principles apply for non-intellectual activities centered on *craft*. When I began learning guitar, I glossed over "minor technique issues" in an attempt to play full songs quickly. Soon, convenient tempo slowdowns near difficult chord changes really held me back. Once I took time to hone basic chord changes and strum patterns, my rate of improvement skyrocketed.

Like many physical crafts, repetitive technique practice can be downright *soul-sucking*, making it tempting to fixate on immediate progress metrics (e.g. time to learn a song). The core issue is that *spending time doing hard things looks unproductive*, and vice versa.

### A practical take

Always slowing down is impractical. Instead, focus on **slowing down the hard and accelerating the easy**. 

Most people distribute time too evenly between easy and hard parts of processes. Ruthlessly optimize typing and computer navigation speed; then, slow down to think deeply about the challenging concepts driving your work. Spend 20% of your time practicing the easy 80% of a musical piece, and 80% drilling the hard 20%.

Slowing down may seem detrimental to your productivity, but it's actually a superpower that lets you:
- deeply understand problems and avoid haphazard solutions,
- break down challenges into simpler pieces that enable immediate action,
- reflect on outcomes to create feedback loops that exponentially accelerate progress, and
- enjoy the process, naturally improving motivation and cognitive function.

Even then, you might argue that you *simply do not have time to slow down*. When I felt this way, the constraint was largely self-imposed. Your immediate first step should be to aggressively eliminate or optimize as much of your busywork as you can – asking "is this actually valuable?" is a good place to start. Given that time buffer, avoid strict time limits for deep work. If you still feel constrained for time, you may be attempting too much to accomplish effectively.

### Conclusion

Learning and working are messy processes involving repeated experience, long-term information processing, and deep flow states. These processes compound over years, which makes optimizing for speed an extremely short-sighted approach. Slowing down is embracing the fact that humans are not productivity machines. We are agents of intuition, creativity, and craft.
