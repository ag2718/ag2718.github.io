Over the past few years, everything in my life has felt fast. As a (self-classified) ambitious person, I’ve always strived to take on more and more. This mentality peaked last year: I took six classes per semester, served as president of my a cappella group, and worked on course staff for two classes. As a result, I hand-built an optimized system for efficient multitasking and getting things done.

The past few months have been different. I’m taking fewer classes and focusing on my research. A lack of constant deadlines has made me more curious, able to focus for longer, and more thoughtful and intentional. This has prompted me to reflect on “slowing down.”

### A mental model

Human brains can’t instantly process information or mechanistically execute tasks; even tasks involving some level of reasoning are already becoming automated by AI. But we are still able to imagine bold and nontrivial solutions to large-scale interdisciplinary problems, largely because of "softer" qualities like creativity, imagination, craft and intuition.

These qualities take years to develop across childhood and adulthood, primarily through the slow accumulation of experiences and thoughts that reshape our brains. Yet there has never been a time in history when we’ve been expected to learn or do things as fast as we are today. While the pursuit of speed is rooted in ambition, it degrades the core human advantage in an attempt to turn us into productivity machines, which we are not suited to be.

### Tasks vs. processes

Human progress, learning, and innovation are made up of *processes*, or the intricate and messy happenings that occur when we learn and do things.  *Tasks* show up on a to-do list. They're either done, or not done, like ticking a checkbox. 

The fundamental issue with "productivity-maxxing" is treating *processes* as *tasks*. This approach works well for filling out a form, but horribly for learning, writing or painting. Unfortunately, the task-oriented mentality is everywhere. Multitasking is commonly associated with high productivity and/or capability. However, it usually entails juggling multiple processes at once just to perform them each poorly.

During particularly stressful weeks of college, I would cycle between assignments, immediately switching problems when the current one required deep thought. I completed my assignments, but often only partially understood the higher-level ideas those very assignments intended to teach me. Being stuck in that mentality led me to undervalue the crucial process of sitting and thinking. 

Now, my slower pace of learning has allowed me to focus on the intuition behind concepts in addition to their mechanics. I often take the time to think about what other approaches would and wouldn't work, which gives me a deeper and more well-rounded understanding of what I learn. This feeling has reminded me a lot of how I felt learning physics in high school, which was motivated by curiosity and exploration rather than a necessity to be "productive." (Learning this way has always been more productive for me in the long run, ironically.)

### Information overload

Learning is one type of crucial process that requires care and attention. Thoughtful learning involves sitting with complex concepts for hours by asking questions, drawing connections, and working on hard problems. By this metric, rapidly consuming information is more akin to productivity doomscrolling than learning.

I realized this in trying to keep up with a wide variety of machine learning research in 2025, skimming countless blogs and research papers and learning almost nothing in the process. In contrast, I've now shifted my focus to fundamental understanding: I'm currently traveling 5+ years into the past and implementing GPT-2 from scratch. While less flashy, this project has improved my understanding of concepts I thought I already understood.

Information consumption is tempting as you don’t have to wrestle with new ideas while skimming an article, watching a video, or scrolling through educational social media posts. Learning quickly offers the illusion of understanding while bypassing all of the messy, slow and complicated processes that build strong foundations.

### Slow and intentional practice makes perfect

While I've primarily talked about intellectual activities, these same principles apply for anything that requires craft or care. When I began learning guitar, I glossed over "minor technique issues" in an attempt to play full songs quickly. Soon, convenient tempo slowdowns near difficult chord changes really held me back. Once I took time to hone basic chord changes and strum patterns, my rate of improvement skyrocketed.

Like many physical crafts, repetitive technique practice can be downright *soul-sucking*, making it tempting to fixate on immediate progress metrics like time to learn a song. The core issue is that spending time doing hard things *looks unproductive* (and vice versa), which is why trying to superficially optimize for productivity is so harmful.

### A practical take

Always slowing down is impractical. However, most people distribute time too evenly between easy and hard parts of processes. Ruthlessly optimizing your typing and computer navigation speed gives you more time to slow down to think deeply about the challenging concepts driving your work. In music, it's common to spend 20% of your time practicing the easy 80% of a piece, and 80% drilling the hard 20%.

Even then, you might argue that you *simply do not have time to slow down*. When I felt this way, the constraint was largely self-imposed. Your immediate first step should be to aggressively eliminate or optimize as much of your busywork as you can – asking "is this actually valuable?" is a good place to start. Given that time buffer, avoid strict time limits for deep work. If you still feel constrained for time, you may be attempting too much to accomplish effectively.

### Conclusion

In addition to slowing down being useful for more thoughtful work and learning, I would argue its most important effect is enabling enjoyment of and engagement with what you're doing. While there are certainly practical constraints, I'm taking these lessons I've learned and trying to slow down when I can, especially as a way to maintain the curiosity and imagination I've known my entire life.
